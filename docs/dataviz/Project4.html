Summary of the Ethical Dilemma and its Applications

The Persistent Socio-Political Dimensions of “Data-Driven” Classification Systems

A persistent question in the study of Data Science, particularly in recent years, has been how to develop and implement data-driven strategies or technologies in ways that promote social justice rather than impede its progress or perpetuate existing inequalities, while acknowledging that data science involves politically consequential choices rather than neutral technical decisions. Data classification systems often reinforce structural inequalities despite claims of neutrality, which persists from historical to contemporary systems.

Bearing a historical context explicitly based in social science, eugenics, and arbitrary predictive algorithms – systems that classify individuals based on physical traits or their material environment must inform the growing strengths and be informed by the proven weaknesses of predictive classification systems —- regardless of intent or contemporary demands.

We must challenge the notion that data scientists can work as neutral technical actors and begin to recognize them as key political actors engaged in normative constructions of society (privacy, media, space, etc.) to evaluate their work according to its increasing downstream impacts on people’s lives. As Ben Green discusses in “Data Science as Political Action,” establishing a guiding theoretical framework is a crucial next step in understanding the past and potential impacts of scientific conclusions or variance tests.

Green argues that all data science work inherently involves political choices and value judgments about how society should function, even when practitioners don’t recognize this dimension of their work. “ON CRIMINAL ANTHROPOMETRY AND THE IDENTIFICATION OF CRIMINALS,” an experiment conducted by W. R. MacDonnell in 1902 as part of Biometrika, demonstrates an early example of how ignorance towards ethical principles and misplaced partiality can depreciate an experiment’s insights and credibility over time.

Using “Head Length, Head Breadth, Face Breadth, Left Middle Finger, Left Cubit, Left Foot, and Height, of each [observed] individual” to classify and identify potential “criminal classes” and genetic (and environmental) predispositions to crime, this early form of data-driven classification attempted to use statistics and arbitrary measurements to distinguish “habitual criminals” from “non-habitual criminals” and differentiate both groups from the general population, demonstrating an early application of using data to categorize (and police) people.

“Machine Bias” by ProPublica provides a much more contemporary case study of these issues through an investigation of the COMPAS algorithm, which is “used to inform decisions about who can be set free at every stage of the criminal justice system, from assigning bond amounts […] to even more fundamental decisions about defendants’ freedom.” ProPublica found that the algorithm incorrectly labeled Black defendants as higher risk for future crimes at nearly twice the rate of white defendants, despite claiming to be objective and fair.

The data science component across these cases involves collecting, analyzing, and making predictions based on data about people, whether through physical measurements in the early 20th century or complex algorithms (sometimes involving physical characteristics) today. The ethical dilemma emerges when these seemingly objective technical processes reproduce or amplify existing social inequalities and biases, particularly when they impact vulnerable populations through criminal justice systems or social services. The challenge becomes how to develop data science practices that actively work toward justice rather than maintaining a veil of neutrality that often preserves the status quo and echoes the oversights of past conclusions through bias.

Questions

What was the data collection process? Were the observations collected ethically? Are there missing observations?

Reviewing ProPublica’s analysis of COMPAS and W. R. MacDonnell’s findings, a troubling pattern of ethical issues has persisted from early anthropometric criminal identification systems to modern algorithmic risk assessment tools, particularly regarding how data is collected.

In MacDonnell’s hypothesis testing, various physical measurements of a randomly sampled group of 3,000 “non-habitual criminals” were recorded—presumably without informed consent. More specifically, the data was provided to him by a colleague from “the Central Metric Office, New Scotland Yard, where the register of habitual criminals is kept, and their identification effected.” He was given direct access to “a number of ‘practice’ forms which record the metric description of a less pronounced type of criminal than the ‘habituals’—prisoners whose crimes and sentences are comparatively slight, and who may be called ‘non-habitual.’”

Non-habitual criminals were distinguished by the perceived severity of their crime rather than its genuine habituality, which could lead to missing or arbitrary observations. Ethically, these components work together to sour the reliability of the data and resultant findings, as they fail to recognize or mitigate any bias whatsoever; include non-high-society-20th-century-UK-academic perspectives (no diversity in the eyes or hands that touched the data); and consider the ethical implications of each decision made with the data.

The COMPAS risk assessment algorithm investigated by ProPublica similarly raises ethical concerns in the data collection process, as it presumably relies on historical crime data (likely from the individual’s record and past cases that share the same area and potentially the individual’s background) that could reflect decades of discriminatory policing practices and biased arrests. It’s also worth noting that Northpointe, COMPAS’ producer, “does not publicly disclose the calculations used to arrive at defendants’ risk scores, so it is not possible for either defendants or the public to see what might be driving the disparity” between the predicted outcomes of Black and White offenders.

Outside of speculation around the content of the data, Northpointe’s refusal to disclose the variables considered by COMPAS’ algorithm signals a failure to “Prioritize the continuous collection and availability of discussions and metadata.” Through ProPublica’s findings (universally higher risk scores for Black individuals), we can assume that they have also failed to recognize and mitigate bias in the data.

“Data Science as Political Action,” an article by Ben Green—an Assistant Professor of Information and (by courtesy) Public Policy at the University of Michigan—frames these issues theoretically, highlighting how data collection processes claiming objectivity often lack democratic participation from affected communities and fail to acknowledge missing observations from marginalized groups. Across all sources, we see a common theme of data collection that lacks transparency, proper consent, and representation of impacted populations, resulting in systems that reproduce historical patterns of discrimination while claiming scientific neutrality through mathematics, whether using early 20th-century anthropometric measurements or contemporary algorithmic decision-making tools.

What was the permission structure for using the data? Was it followed?

In MacDonnell’s study, no meaningful consent process appears to be documented for those measured and categorized, reflecting an era when ethical standards for research on human subjects were minimal, particularly for prisoners or those deemed “criminal classes.” The contemporary COMPAS algorithm examined by ProPublica represents a modern variant of this issue, where risk assessments are mandatory in many jurisdictions (COMPAS is used in courts in Arizona, Colorado, Delaware, Kentucky, Louisiana, Oklahoma, Virginia, Washington, and Wisconsin), with defendants having no option to opt out despite the profound consequences these assessments have on their lives. Northpointe claims proprietary protection over its algorithm, preventing transparency while collecting sensitive personal data from individuals with little choice.

The “Data Science as Political Action” article once again contextualizes these permission issues theoretically, arguing that the fundamental problem is the lack of democratic consent and participation - data subjects remain objects of analysis rather than participants with agency in how their data is used. All three sources demonstrate a consistent theme of power imbalance in data collection and usage, where those with the least power have their data collected and analyzed with minimal consent, transparency, or recourse.

Who was measured? Are those individuals representative of the people to whom we’d like to generalize / apply the algorithm?

“ON CRIMINAL ANTHROPOMETRY AND THE IDENTIFICATION OF CRIMINALS” explicitly targets individuals already labeled as “criminals,” creating a circular logic where physical measurements are used to identify a “criminal class” based on data from those already in the system, making generalizations inherently biased. Similarly, the COMPAS algorithm investigated by ProPublica is trained on historical criminal justice data reflecting decades of discriminatory policing practices, disproportionately measuring Black defendants while claiming to predict recidivism fairly across populations.

This pattern connects directly to the theoretical framework in “Data Science as Political Action,” which critiques how data science often measures marginalized populations who become subjects of surveillance and control, while those with power remain unmeasured and unscrutinized. The consistent theme across historical and contemporary examples is that measurement tends to focus on already disadvantaged groups, creating systems that claim objective generalizability while actually reproducing and reinforcing existing social hierarchies and biases—the very people subjected to these measurements are rarely representative of the general population, yet the resulting algorithms are applied broadly as though they capture universal patterns of human behavior.

Should race be used as a variable? Is it a proxy for something else?

A central tension across all three references revolves around the use of race (or a proxy for race) as a variable in data systems, revealing how historical prejudices persist in modern algorithms. The 1902 criminal anthropometry study, although not explicitly mentioning race, utilized physical measurements that often served as proxies for racial categorization during an era steeped in eugenics and scientific racism, creating taxonomies of “criminal types” that reinforced racial stereotypes.

The COMPAS algorithm exemplifies the contemporary manifestation of this issue; while it doesn’t directly use race as a variable (to our knowledge), it likely incorporates factors such as criminal history, neighborhood, and family background that serve as proxies for race in a society shaped by systemic inequality, leading to Black defendants being falsely labeled high-risk at nearly twice the rate of white defendants.

Once again providing theoretical context for this pattern, “Data Science as Political Action” argues that seemingly “neutral” variables often encode racial bias, and that treating race as merely a technical variable overlooks how data systems participate in constructing and reproducing racial categories with real social consequences. Across centuries, these cases illustrate how data systems – using both calipers and computers – continue to rely on direct or proxy measurements that entrench racial hierarchies while claiming scientific objectivity.

Conclusion

The evolution of data-driven systems from early 20th-century anthropometric measurements to modern algorithmic risk assessment tools reveals ethical challenges that persist despite superficial fantasies of numerical partiality and bipartisanship.

Despite the increasing sophistication of data science methodologies, fundamental issues remain unresolved: problematic data collection processes that lack meaningful consent; permission structures that disempower those being measured; measurement practices that disproportionately target marginalized populations; and the continued use of race or its proxies in ways that perpetuate historical inequities.

These sources collectively demonstrate that the ethical dilemmas in data science cannot be resolved through technical solutions alone. Professor Ben Green’s framework offers a crucial perspective — that data scientists must recognize themselves as political actors whose work inevitably shapes social outcomes. Whether in the criminal anthropometry of 1902 or the risk assessment algorithms of today, data-driven systems have never been neutral technical tools; they are powerful mechanisms that can either challenge or reinforce existing power structures.

Moving forward requires more than ethical guidelines or algorithmic adjustments; it demands a fundamental reimagining of data science practice that centers justice (for a comprehensive application, a broad redefinition of ‘justice’ is likely needed), incorporates democratic participation from affected communities, and acknowledges the inherently political nature of classifying and predicting human behavior. Without this shift in approach, even the most advanced data science technologies risk perpetuating the same patterns of exclusion and discrimination that have characterized their historical predecessors.
