The Persistent Socio-Political Dimensions of “Data-Driven” Classification Systems

A persistent question in the study of Data Science, particularly in more recent years, has been: how to develop and implent data-driven strategies or technologies in ways that promote social justice rather than impeding its progress or perpetuating existing inequalities – while acknowledging that data science inherently involves political choices rather than neutral technical decisions.

Bearing historical context that is based specifically in social “science”, eugenics, and predictive algorithms – systems that classify individuals based on physical traits or their material environment must be informed by the potential strengths (as sen in reference 2) and proven weaknesses (as seen in reference 1 and reference 3) of predictive classification systems – regardless of intent.

It is essential that we challenge the notion that data scientists can work as neutral technical actors, and begin to recognize them as key political actors engaged in normative constructions of society (privacy, media, space, etc.) to evaluate their work according to its downstream impacts on people’s lives. As done by Ben Green in “Data Science as Political Action,” establishing a guiding theoretical framework is a key next step in beginning to understand the past and potential impacts of scientific conclusions or variance tests.

Green argues that all data science work inherently involves political choices and value judgments about how society should function, even when practitioners don’t recognize this dimension of their work. “ON CRIMINAL ANTHROPOMETRY AND THE IDENTIFICATION OF CRIMINALS,” an experiment conducted by W. R. MacDonnell in 1902 as part of Biometrika, laregely used skull measurements to classify and identify potential “criminal classes” or genetic (and environmental) predispositions to crime. This early form of data-driven classification attempted to use statistics and measurements to differentiate criminals from the general population, demonstrating an early application of using data to categorize people – and to police them in particular.

“Machine Bias” by ProPublica provides a much more contemporary case study of these issues through an investigation of the COMPAS algorithm, a risk assessment tool used in the criminal justice system. ProPublica found that the algorithm incorrectly labeled Black defendants as higher risk for future crimes at nearly twice the rate of white defendants, despite claiming to be objective and fair.

The data science component across these sources involves collecting, analyzing, and making predictions based on data about people - whether through physical measurements in the early 20th century or complex algorithms (sometimes involving physical characteristics) today. The ethical dilemma emerges when these seemingly objective technical processes reproduce or amplify existing social inequalities and biases, particularly when they impact vulnerable populations through criminal justice systems or social services. The challenge becomes how to develop data science practices that actively work toward justice rather than maintaining an impossible neutrality that often preserves the status quo and echoes the oversights of past conclusions.

Reviewing Propublika’s analysis of COMPA and W. R. MacDonnell’s findings, there’s a troubling pattern of ethical issues that have persisted from the early anthropometric criminal identification systems to modern algorithmic risk assessment tools. In the 1902 criminal anthropometry work, data was collected through physical measurements of individuals presumably without informed consent, with implicit biases in categorizing “criminal classes” versus “normal” populations.
